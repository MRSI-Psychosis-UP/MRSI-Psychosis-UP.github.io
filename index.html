Research

Translational research into the mechanisms of psychosis.

The primary mission of the Psychosis Unit is to better understand the neurobiological mechanisms underlying psychosis, in order to improve the care of adolescents and young adults suffering from schizophrenia-spectrum disorders or other psychotic disorders.

Since its creation in 2022, our Unit conducts "translational" research. This means that our work draws both on clinical data from patients participating in our studies (for example, blood markers or symptom severity) and on laboratory experiments (for example, experimental disease models using cell cultures). This approach has been applied for over a decade at the Centre for Psychiatric Neuroscience; it was introduced by Kim Do (former head of the Unit for Research in Schizophrenia), in collaboration with the clinical services of the Department of Psychiatry, and has since developed progressively with the support of the NCCR-Synapsy.

We are also strongly committed to training a new generation of young clinician-researchers, some of whom follow an MD-PhD program. Clinician-researchers are essential for bridging the gap between clinical practice and fundamental research, and for maintaining a dialogue between healthcare professionals and patients on one hand, and neuroscientists on the other.

The Unit is therefore composed of a multidisciplinary team that includes both scientists (biologists, a physicist, a biochemist) and clinicians (physicians, psychologists, an art therapist).

Team

The people of the Psychosis Unit.

Paul Klauser

Head of Unit
  • Child and adolescent psychiatrist and psychotherapist, MD-PhD

Paul Klauser is a senior psychiatrist in the University Service of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (SUPEA) at CHUV, where he leads the Psychosis Unit. After a PhD in neurobiology on synaptic plasticity and dendritic spine dynamics during brain development, he turned to neuroimaging (structural, diffusion and spectroscopic MRI) to bridge microscopic findings and the young patients he treats.

His work has shown that white matter alterations in schizophrenia converge on brain network hubs, and that N-acetylcysteine, a precursor of the brain antioxidant glutathione, can restore white matter integrity in the fornix of patients with early psychosis. He has been active in the Research Academy of the European Society for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (ESCAP) since 2016 and collaborates with research groups across Europe and Australia.

Daniela Dwir-Giangreco

Project Leader
  • Biologist, PhD

Daniela Dwir-Giangreco studies how genetic and environmental risk factors interact to cause oxidative stress and neuroinflammation during sensitive periods of brain development, and how this disrupts parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory interneurons and the excitatory/inhibitory balance seen in schizophrenia.

Working across induced pluripotent stem cell models, animal models and patient cohorts, she identified a vicious circle of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation driven by MMP9/RAGE. In patients in the early phase of psychosis, raised blood RAGE levels were linked to lower prefrontal GABA and improved with antioxidant supplementation, making MMP9/RAGE a promising biomarker and therapeutic target.

Pascal Steullet

Head of Research
  • Biologist, PhD

Pascal Steullet investigates the mechanisms behind the altered brain connectivity of schizophrenia, a neurodevelopmental disorder. His work showed how redox dysregulation and oxidative stress impair NMDA receptors, synaptic plasticity and fast-spiking parvalbumin interneurons, and, with Jan-Harry Cabungcal, that the perineuronal net surrounding these neurons protects them against oxidative stress.

More recently he has turned to the thalamus, using multimodal MRI to characterise thalamic abnormalities across the stages of psychosis and to look for imaging markers that precede a first psychotic episode.

Centre for Psychiatric Neuroscience

The Psychosis Unit is part of the Department of Psychiatry and the Centre for Psychiatric Neuroscience at CHUV, the University Hospital of Lausanne. Our translational approach was introduced at the Centre over a decade ago by Kim Do, former head of the Unit for Research in Schizophrenia, in collaboration with the clinical services of the Department of Psychiatry, and has since grown with the support of the NCCR-Synapsy.

CHUV — University Hospital of Lausanne
